Executive Summary

CONTEXT — 10 JULY 2026: The war that began on 28 February 2026 with the US–Israeli strikes and the assassination of Ali Khamenei did not end the Islamic Republic. Mojtaba Khamenei was installed as the third ‘Supreme Leader’ on 9 March. Ali Larijani, the system’s de-facto wartime leader, was assassinated on 17 March. The mass uprising that Washington and Tel Aviv predicted never came. A Pakistan-mediated ceasefire took hold on 8 April and was formalized on 17 June, when the presidents of the United States and Iran signed a memorandum of understanding to end the war. It did not hold: after an Iranian drone strike on a commercial vessel in the Strait of Hormuz, US forces struck some ninety Iranian targets, and on 8 July President Trump declared the ceasefire “over.” Strikes are being traded as this document goes out, while Pakistan and Qatar work to bring both sides back to the table. Ali Khamenei’s state funeral was held on 6–7 July. Inside Iran, the internet has been only partially restored since 25 May — roughly 40% of pre-war traffic, under a whitelist system with tiered, IRGC-linked “Internet Pro” access.

The March 2026 draft of this document was written in the first week of the war, when a rapid collapse of the system appeared possible and this project positioned itself as “day-after” constitutional infrastructure. That moment did not arrive. This edition is repositioned accordingly — and honestly.

What the war proved is our founding argument. Iranians possess immense political energy, expertise, and desire for change — yet when the most violent shock in the regime’s history decapitated its leadership, society had no mechanism to convert that energy into coordinated action. Loud but unrepresentative social media, easily manipulated polls, a fragmented diaspora, intense surveillance, and an opposition waiting for a “final call” that never came. Energy without mechanisms leads to stagnation. Democracies do not fail because people stop wanting freedom; they fail because, when the moment of change arrives, society lacks the plumbing — verified participation, structured deliberation, trusted coordination, institutional emergence.

Digital Republic (Jomhoor) is that plumbing: organisation & referendum-readiness infrastructure, built before the next moment arrives. It does not claim to run a binding national referendum under unfree conditions. It moves citizens through a progressive pipeline — self-understanding & organizing in groups and coalitions (Civic Compass), deliberating (Taraaz), public sensing (transparent opinion measurement), and deciding (referendum readiness) via Jomhoor wallet— so that when a democratic moment arrives, society is well organized and actually knows what it is voting on.

Since March, the platform has moved from prototype to a working, partly audited stack:

  1. Verified, private identity — zero-knowledge proofs from Iranian national ID cards and biometric passports; circuits audited by Halborn; ZKML likeness & liveness; one person = one reusable DID; no personal data ever leaves the device.
  2. Live civic tools — Taraaz (deliberation, built on Agora & Pol.is) and Civic Compass are deployed in production; the INID Assistant is published on Google Play and the Apple App Store; the Jomhoor Wallet provides single sign-on across the ecosystem.
  3. Structural safeguards — a “Two Gates” model separating identity verification from deliberation, a normative compliance layer aligned with UN human-rights instruments, and a Jomhoor-owned sovereign trust root (the M6 stack) on Rarimo L2.

We are seeking ~$1.6 million for Year 1 to fund a 10-person core team in two phases: a 3-month initialization phase (~$309,000) with 7 key hires, scaling to full operations. At steady state, the Civic Sponsorship model targets 9,000 recurring donors at $10/month — $1.08M/year, covering roughly two-thirds of the ~$1.8M steady-state budget without any state sponsor. The remainder is designed to come from Web3 public-goods grants, European funds, and the hybrid revenue model (“Login with Jomhoor” identity services and the Movement Bank). Full donor coverage would require ~14,000 donors — a target we work toward, not the base case we plan on.

What the War Proved

The regime survived decapitation. The regime absorbed the assassination of its Supreme Leader, dozens of senior commanders, and its de facto wartime leader — and consolidated. Succession was settled inside the system, in wartime, by the Assembly of Experts. “Regime change from the air” failed exactly as US intelligence predicted it would.

The uprising never came — and not because Iranians accept the system. The December 2025–January 2026 protests, and the mass killings that followed, demonstrated overwhelming demand for change. What was missing was not courage but mechanism: no verified way to know what the population actually wants, no structured way to deliberate alternatives, no coordination layer the fragmented opposition could trust, and no leadership that could credibly claim a mandate. The opposition’s most prominent figure who had once asked people to come to the streets and said help is underway, asked them to wait at home.

Connectivity is now an instrument of rule. The regime emerged from the war’s main phase with a two-tier internet: a whitelist for the population, privileged “Internet Pro” access for those who can pay operators tied to the IRGC. Digital participation tools that assume an open internet are naive; tools designed for repression — offline verification, circumvention integration, low bandwidth — are not.

The window will reopen. The ceasefire’s collapse on 8 July — three weeks after it was signed — proves it was a pause, not a peace. The new Supreme Leader is injured, governs by statement, and inherits a shattered command structure, a collapsed currency, and a population that has twice in six months shown its readiness to move. The question is not whether another moment of change arrives. The question is whether, next time, the mechanisms exist.

The Repositioning: Referendum-Readiness Infrastructure

What we are not

  • We are not a crisis-management tool. The war confirmed this: during bombardment and blackout, security actors dominate. We do not operate in that phase and never claimed to.
  • We do not claim to run binding referenda under unfree conditions. A real referendum requires universal suffrage, voter safety, free media, independent election authorities, and observation. None of these exist in Iran today. Pretending otherwise would destroy the platform’s credibility — honest limits create more legitimacy than exaggerated claims.
  • We are not a political faction. We do not endorse any exile organization, or any foreign power’s vision for Iran. The platform’s legitimacy comes from verified Iranian citizens, not from Washington, Tel Aviv, Riyadh, or any self-appointed council.

What we are

Referendum-readiness infrastructure — the connective tissue that moves citizens through a progressive democratic pipeline, so that democratic capacity exists before it is needed:

  1. Self-understanding — Civic Compass: citizens map their priorities and values across policy dimensions and achieve civic self-understanding.
  2. Organizing — matchmaking connects complementary profiles into groups and coalitions via compass-vector similarity rather than social-media popularity, with mutual-consent E2E-encrypted contact.
  3. Deliberating — Taraaz: structured debate turns vague slogans into comparable, defined policy programs; disagreement is not the enemy of democracy — the absence of mechanisms to manage it is.
  4. Public sensing — transparent opinion measurement that publishes its uncertainty instead of overclaiming.
  5. Deciding — referendum readiness: educating voters, testing ballot formats, running transparent trial votes, and comparing deliberative outcomes — so a genuine referendum can be executed the moment conditions allow.

Design principles

The platform operationalizes seven theoretical commitments (set out fully in the June 2026 framework paper):

  1. Infrastructure before events — build the pipes before the crisis; pre-transition democratic engineering, not political opposition.
  2. Privacy as democratic design — verified without being visible, unique without being known; in an authoritarian context privacy is a safety condition, not a luxury.
  3. Deliberation precedes decision — opinions are the input to deliberation, not the output of democracy.
  4. Differentiated access, equal standing — the route into the democratic field never determines standing within it; the DID carries no trace of how a citizen arrived.
  5. Democracy as rehearsal — authoritarian societies atrophy democratic capacity; every structured deliberation rebuilds democratic muscle memory.
  6. Proof of personhood as legitimacy — before asking what people think, prove a unique, real human is doing the thinking.
  7. Political community without territory — a verified, deliberating, decision-capable demos assembled across Iran and its diaspora simultaneously, without any government’s permission.

Identity & privacy architecture

  • Self-sovereign DID — generated locally on the user’s device, never on a server; one DID per verified human; reused across all features; only cryptographic proofs are stored — no PII.
  • Zero-knowledge verification — Noir and Circom circuit implementations, audited by Halborn; supports all four biometric passport types relevant to the target population and multiple national ID card series.
  • ZKML liveness & likeness check — on-device machine learning verifies liveness and face-likeness against the document (86% match threshold); biometric data never leaves the device. Published false-accept and false-reject rates will accompany this threshold in the security documentation.
  • Censorship resilience — integration with circumvention tools (such as Psiphon) is part of the core implementation architecture, a direct answer to the whitelist internet.
  • Sovereign trust root (M6) — Jomhoor uses the speed and security of the Rarimo L2 chain while owning its identity contracts (RegistrationSMT, CertificatesSMT), governed multisig admin keys, ICAO root snapshots, and dispatcher support for Iranian documents. Sovereignty without running a proprietary blockchain.

Defending against bots and platform capture: the Two Gates

Identity proves uniqueness, not democratic intent. The platform therefore maintains two structural defenses:

  • The Identity Gate — ZK proofs and nullifiers stop bots, Sybils, and low-cost foreign interference; ensures unique Iranians (it cannot stop a real citizen acting in bad faith — no honest system can).
  • The Speech & Process Gate — human-rights frameworks, appeals, and independent oversight stop platform capture, incitement, and atrocity denial while protecting lawful political disagreement. Token-weighted voting and “pay-to-play” are explicitly banned.

Sustainability: Funding Model

Pillar 1 — The Citizen Tax (recurring micro-payments)

The Iranian diaspora holds enormous economic capital; its political impotence is a failure of capital conversion, not resources.

  • The ask: $10/month — the price of one lunch.
  • The math: 9,000 recurring donors × $10/month = $1.08M/year — roughly two-thirds of steady-state operations; full donor coverage would require ~14,000.
  • The realism: recurring-donation conversion typically runs 5–10% of an engaged audience, so 9,000 donors implies 100,000+ engaged supporters. We treat the Citizen Tax as a target, not the base case — sustainability is designed across all three pillars plus Horizon-2 revenue, so no single pillar failing is fatal.
  • The principle: One person, one vote. Diaspora sponsors earn one governance credit and social badges; residents inside Iran claim credits free from the Solidarity Pool with equal rights. Donors get badges, not extra votes — the rich cannot buy the Republic.
  • Channels: crypto (ETH/Polygon/Rarimo, BTC, Solana), PayPal, Patreon.

Pillar 2 — Web3 public-goods funding

ZK identity, privacy-preserving voting, and censorship-resistant deliberation are public goods for the entire open internet. Targets: Gitcoin Grants, Optimism RetroPGF, Ethereum Foundation. Non-political capital supporting the codebase without political strings.

Pillar 3 — The European municipal track

The German sister-city pilot qualifies for EU innovation grants and German federal research funds. Shared codebase: European funds for European digital democracy directly upgrade the Iranian platform — fully legal and compliant.

Horizon 2 — Hybrid revenue model

  • “Login with Jomhoor” (B2B identity-as-a-service): privacy-preserving SSO and API for relying parties (NGOs, research panels, civic apps). Jomhoor acts as a privacy buffer — partners receive verified proofs of uniqueness (nullifiers, ZK-verified assertions, pairwise pseudonymous subjects via OAuth2/PKCE), never raw identity data. Monetized through tiered verification credits.
  • The Movement Bank: participation-linked contributions — proposal sponsorship, civic bounties, verified crowdfunding for audited civic projects. Money executes mandates but grants no political weight; token-weighted voting is banned.
  • Growth phases: Pilot (grants and partner-funded SSO), then Scale (Movement Bank, sponsorship and bounty modules), then Resilience (self-sustaining via verification credits and transaction fees).

Assumptions & Transparency

What we know:

  • The Islamic Republic survived the 2026 war and is consolidating under Mojtaba Khamenei. The 17 June memorandum collapsed on 8 July; strikes have resumed, and mediation efforts continue. The war is not over.
  • The internet inside Iran is partially restored, censored, and tiered. Open participation from inside Iran is constrained but not impossible; diaspora participation is unconstrained.
  • The platform’s core components are built, live, and audited: identity circuits (Halborn), Taraaz, Civic Compass, wallet/SSO, published mobile apps.

What we assume:

  • Another window of change will open. Succession fragility, economic collapse, and demonstrated mass demand for change make this an assumption of timing, not possibility.
  • Democratic capacity can be built in advance — through diaspora participation, circumvention-enabled participation inside Iran, and structured rehearsal.
  • Civil-society organizations will adopt shared infrastructure rather than fragmenting across incompatible tools.

What we cannot control:

  • Whether and when a transition comes, or what triggers it.
  • Whether any faction adopts the platform. We can build the best tools; we cannot force adoption.
  • Whether the renewed fighting escalates into full-scale war or returns to negotiation.

What this means for sponsors: your funding builds infrastructure with value in every scenario: if a democratic opening comes, this is the only operational readiness layer; if it does not, the technology serves as an open-source public good — deployed in Europe, available to any population that needs it — and Iranian civil society gains verified deliberation, honest opinion measurement, and democratic rehearsal in the meantime.
